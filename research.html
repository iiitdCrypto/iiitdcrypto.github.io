01

Classical Cryptanalysis

The security of a block or stream cipher is an empirical claim. It holds until someone finds the structure that breaks it, and the only way to know how much margin a design really has is to attack it.

We are interested in complete attacks as well as distinguishers, and in attack models that match what an adversary can actually do.

Current threads

  • Fault attacks under realistic models. Differential fault analysis where the injection assumptions reflect what is achievable on a device, not what is convenient for the analysis.
  • Structural attacks on Feistel constructions. Algebraic and meet-in-the-middle techniques against generalized Feistel networks, and how the attacks scale with the number of branches.
  • The real cost of brute force. Benchmarking exhaustive key search on GPUs across a range of symmetric ciphers, to put a measured number under the phrase "computationally infeasible".
02

Quantum Cryptanalysis

Grover's algorithm is usually quoted as halving the security of a symmetric cipher. That figure is an asymptotic bound, not a cost. Written out as a circuit, an attack has a gate count, a depth, a qubit budget, and a set of memory assumptions — and those are what decide whether it is a threat.

Our position is that quantum attacks should be costed at circuit level, without granting the adversary free quantum memory access.

Current threads

  • Circuit-level resource estimates. Grover- and Simon-based attacks accounted honestly, without assuming unit-cost quantum RAM.
  • The query-access boundary. Which attacks survive when the adversary has only classical access to the primitive, rather than superposition queries.
  • Quantum time–memory–data tradeoffs. What the classical tradeoff curves for stream ciphers become once the search step is quantum.
  • Quantumization of classical cryptanalysis. Lifting classical cryptanalysis techniques into the quantum setting, and finding where the speedup genuinely applies.
03

Design of Symmetric Ciphers

Homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation changed what makes a cipher cheap. Multiplicative depth and the number of nonlinear operations now dominate the cost, where gate count and table lookups once did.

That shift produced a family of new primitives, many defined over prime fields, whose security is far less settled than that of a cipher like AES. Both halves of that — building them and breaking them — are open.

Current threads

  • Low-depth constructions. Block and stream cipher designs for FHE- and MPC-friendly settings, where multiplicative depth is the budget that matters.
  • Arithmetization-oriented primitives. Security analysis of ciphers defined over prime fields, where the usual binary-field intuitions do not transfer.
  • Mapping the fault surface. Systematising how fault attacks behave against this family, which responds quite differently from conventional designs.
04

Machine Learning for Cryptanalysis

In some settings a neural network separates cipher output from random better than the best hand-built distinguisher. The interesting question is not that this works but what the network has found.

We care about turning learned distinguishers into full key-recovery attacks, and about reading back the structure they rely on in terms a cryptanalyst can state.

Current threads

  • From distinguisher to key recovery. Building complete attacks on top of learned distinguishers.
  • Localising key dependence. Working out which key bits a learned distinguisher is actually sensitive to, and using that to narrow the search.
  • Learning-guided fault analysis. Searching for effective fault strategies automatically instead of designing each one by hand.
  • Interpretability. Extracting a classical explanation from a trained model, so a result is understood and not just observed.
